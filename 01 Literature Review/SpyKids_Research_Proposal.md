# Abstract

Over 100 million children worldwide are estimated to be growing up separated from at least one parent who has migrated for work — a phenomenon known in the migration and public-health literature as being "left behind." While there is a large and consistent evidence base showing that these children face elevated risk of anxiety, depression and behavioural difficulty, comparatively little of that evidence has been translated into evaluated, everyday, non-clinical interaction design responses — particularly ones that centre the child's own experience rather than the migrant parent's.

This proposal, developed by the SpyKids team, sets out a research programme investigating how parental absence affects the emotional wellbeing, guidance, and development of left-behind children specifically within low-income, financially disadvantaged labour-migrant households, and what is actually needed to address this gap. This focus reflects a deliberate scope decision: while the team's Phase 1 framing initially treated financial-difficulty status as one of two parallel segments to test, the literature review and subsequent team discussion confirmed that financial hardship shapes a materially different problem profile — compounding time/companionship gaps with security, supervision, and education-access vulnerabilities not present to the same degree in financially stable households. The project therefore narrows its primary research focus to this segment, while retaining findings on financially stable households as contextual comparison rather than a co-equal focus.

Building on Phase 1 problem-identification work and a literature review of nine peer-reviewed sources, the team has identified communication quality and caregiving quality — operating alongside, rather than instead of, financial hardship — as the strongest evidenced levers for a design intervention. Using a triangulated qualitative Suite of Methods (contextual inquiry, rapid ethnography, digital ethnography, mapping, object inventory and autoethnography) with adult intermediaries — caregivers, educators and community workers — rather than direct child participants, the team will gather 6–10 participant engagements across Weeks 4–5, prioritising financially disadvantaged migrant households, to test the assumptions documented in the Problem Identification baseline before any design concept is proposed.

The research is positioned not to prove that parental absence affects wellbeing, which is already well evidenced, nor to build a clinical intervention, which sits outside the team's expertise, but to explore how everyday interaction design can support the quality and meaning of parent–child connection across distance for children in economically disadvantaged migrant households specifically, for the child rather than only the parent, while treating the on-the-ground caregiver as a legitimate third party in that relationship rather than an obstacle to route around.

Key Words: Left-Behind Children, Interaction Design, Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), Migration, Parent-Child Communication, Vulnerable Populations, Qualitative Research.

# 1. Introduction

Left-behind children (LBC) — children who remain in their place of origin while one or both parents migrate elsewhere for work — represent one of the largest and least visible populations affected by global labour migration. Global estimates place the number of LBC above 100 million, concentrated in low- and middle-income countries across Asia, Eastern Europe, Africa and Latin America. Despite this scale, the interaction design response to their experience remains thin: most existing evidence documents the problem (elevated psychological risk) rather than evaluating design solutions, and what design-relevant work does exist is concentrated in a narrow geographic and cultural context.

The SpyKids team is investigating this space as part of DECO2850/DECO7285 Studio 2, working toward an interaction design response grounded in evidence rather than assumption. This proposal formalises the team's Phase 1 problem framing and Phase 2 literature review into a single research plan, and sets out the implementation approach — methodology, schedule, resourcing, risk, and ethics — that will carry the project from problem understanding through to a design concept hypothesis.

## 1.1. Research Questions

### 1.1.1. Primary Question

How does parental absence affect left-behind children's emotional wellbeing, guidance, and development — and what is actually needed to address this gap?

### 1.1.2. Secondary Questions

- **Who (Stakeholders):** Who is affected, directly and indirectly, by a parent's labour migration, and how does the on-the-ground caregiver's role and communication style shape the child's day-to-day emotional experience?
- **How (Form of Separation):** What forms does the separation take — duration, distance, communication frequency, caregiving arrangement — and which communication patterns do children and caregivers themselves describe as meaningful versus hollow?
- **Why (Drivers):** Within households where migration is driven by financial necessity rather than aspirational betterment, how does that underlying economic pressure shape the quality and consistency of the parent–child relationship that is maintained across distance?
- **Impact (Effects):** What downstream effects are observed on wellbeing, guidance, academic outcomes, and family relationships — and specifically, which everyday moments of guidance and connection do children and caregivers describe as missing or difficult?
- **Risks (Assumptions):** Where might the team's assumptions about "left-behind children" as a uniform group break down, and where might a well-intentioned technology intervention create new burdens (surveillance, guilt, disrupted routines) rather than relieve existing ones?

## 1.2. Problem Statement

Children in low-income, financially disadvantaged labour-migrant households who are left behind when a parent migrates for work face a compounded set of risks: reduced day-to-day companionship and guidance, diminished supervision and safety oversight (particularly where care shifts to grandparents or extended family), constrained access to quality education, and a heightened risk of unnoticed emotional distress — all compounding on top of the parent–child separation itself. Existing evidence robustly documents these harms at a population level, but offers little in the way of evaluated, everyday, child-centred design responses that could plausibly support the quality of connection between these children and their migrant parents, or ease the burden placed on the on-the-ground caregiver who mediates that connection.

This is deliberately kept high-level here as the team's shared working problem statement going into Phase 2 fieldwork. Each team member, including the author, will develop their own detailed problem framing — specific user personas, settings, and a proposed design direction — as part of their individual Assessment 1 submission, using this proposal as its evidentiary and scope baseline.

## 1.3. Research Context and Significance

Current understanding of the left-behind-children experience is characterised by a similar trade-off to many public-health literatures: broad epidemiological evidence of harm, but comparatively little translation into evaluated, design-relevant response. A large-scale systematic review and meta-analysis of 111 studies covering close to 265,000 children found that LBC in low- and middle-income countries carry significantly higher risk of depression, anxiety, conduct disorder, substance use and self-harm than children living with both parents (Fellmeth et al., 2018). Earlier clinical-style studies in China found abnormally high rates of emotional and behavioural problems among LBC compared with peers living with both parents (Fan et al., 2010), and a cross-cultural review of maternal migration found that separation from a mother is associated with markedly higher reports of loneliness, anger and anxiety, consistent with attachment-theory explanations of the parent-child bond (Al-Azzeh & Diab, 2025).

This significance is not uniform, however. The literature does not treat LBC as a homogeneous group — findings vary meaningfully by communication quality, caregiving arrangement, financial status, and age (Xie et al., 2024; Račaitė et al., 2021). This variability is central to the SpyKids team's framing: the project's Phase 1 "Risks" lens explicitly flags the danger of assuming a uniform experience across ages, genders and cultural contexts, and the literature review has since confirmed this is not merely a theoretical risk but a well-evidenced feature of the population.

The research is significant to the field of interaction design specifically because the existing intervention literature is outcome-heavy and solution-light: it is dominated by epidemiological and psychological outcome studies and comparatively thin on evaluated, everyday, non-clinical support tools — particularly ones designed around the child's own agency rather than the parent's or the researcher's (see Section 2.5). The SpyKids project is positioned to help close this gap.

## 1.4. Research Scope

To keep the project technically and ethically feasible within a 16-week studio timeline, the team has defined an explicit boundary around what this research will and will not investigate. The team's central scope decision is a narrowing from the two parallel segments identified in Phase 1 (with/without financial difficulty) to a primary focus on left-behind children in **low-income, financially disadvantaged labour-migrant households**, on the basis that this segment carries a compounded and distinctly evidenced problem profile (Section 2.3).

### 1.4.1 In-Scope

- Left-behind children in low-income, financially disadvantaged labour-migrant households specifically, where migration is driven primarily by economic necessity rather than aspirational betterment.
- Emotional wellbeing, guidance, and everyday parent–child connection during separation within this segment, aligned to the primary research question.
- Children broadly aged 6–17, recognising the literature's finding that risk and needs differ meaningfully by age band.
- The compounded problem profile specific to financially disadvantaged households: time/companionship gaps alongside security, supervision, and education-access vulnerabilities (Section 3.2 of the Problem Identification Document).
- The communication and caregiving relationship as a primary design lever within this segment, consistent with the literature review's finding that this remains a strong evidenced moderating factor even where financial hardship is present.
- Secondary literature, adult-intermediary interviews (caregivers, educators, NGO/community workers), and online ethnography, per the team's ethical strategy for a vulnerable population.
- A primary cultural/geographic anchor to be agreed by the team, with awareness that most existing evidence is China-centred.

### 1.4.2 Out-of-Scope

- Left-behind children in households **without** financial difficulty, where migration is driven primarily by aspirational betterment rather than economic necessity — retained in the literature review as contextual comparison (Section 2.3) but not a target of primary data collection.
- Clinical diagnosis, treatment, or therapy for depression, anxiety or other mental-health conditions.
- Infants and toddlers (under 6), and adult "left-behind" family members (e.g. left-behind spouses or elderly parents), which fall under a related but distinct body of literature.
- Household income generation, remittance management, or migration-policy advocacy as ends in themselves — real factors shaping the households studied, but outside an interaction-design response.
- Separation due to divorce, incarceration, deployment, or forced displacement — related but distinct literatures with different dynamics.
- Direct primary research contact with children as research participants, given the ethical sensitivity of a vulnerable minor population.
- Claiming findings are generalisable to left-behind children broadly, rather than specifically to the financially disadvantaged segment studied.

This scope is treated as a live boundary, revisited at the Phase 2 synthesis checkpoint once fieldwork findings are available; any further change is a deliberate team decision, not scope drift.

# 2. Background and Literature Review

This review draws on peer-reviewed systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and primary studies to test each element of the team's Phase 1 framing against existing evidence, and to identify where an interaction design contribution is warranted rather than assumed.

## 2.1 Prevalence and Definition

"Left-behind children" is an established term in migration and public-health literature, referring to children who remain in their place of origin while one or both parents migrate for work, typically for six months or longer. The phenomenon is large in scale: global estimates put the number of LBC above 100 million, concentrated in low- and middle-income countries across Asia, Eastern Europe, Africa and Latin America. Most of the existing evidence base is drawn from internal rural-to-urban migration in China, with a smaller but growing body of work on international labour migration — for example the Philippines, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Ghana, and Eastern Europe. This concentration is an important scope consideration for the team, addressed in Section 1.4.

## 2.2 Emotional and Psychological Impacts

A large-scale systematic review and meta-analysis of 111 studies covering close to 265,000 children found that left-behind children in low- and middle-income countries carry a significantly higher risk of depression, anxiety, conduct disorder, substance use and self-harm than children living with both parents (Fellmeth et al., 2018). A separate systematic review focused specifically on international labour migration reached a similar conclusion, though it noted that outcomes vary meaningfully by region and are not uniformly negative — some studies report improved material wellbeing offsetting emotional cost, particularly where remittances materially raise household living standards (Antia et al., 2020).

Earlier clinical-style studies in China found abnormally high rates of emotional and behavioural problems among LBC compared with peers living with both parents (Fan et al., 2010), and a cross-cultural review of maternal migration specifically found that separation from a mother is associated with markedly higher reports of loneliness, anger and anxiety, consistent with attachment-theory explanations of the parent-child bond (Al-Azzeh & Diab, 2025). These findings directly validate the "Impact" lens from Phase 1 and confirm emotional wellbeing as a well-evidenced, non-speculative problem space.

## 2.3 Moderating and Protective Factors

The literature does not treat LBC as a homogeneous group, which directly supports the Phase 1 "Risks" flag about assuming uniformity of experience. Several factors consistently moderate outcomes:

- **Communication quality and frequency:** a large study of Chinese LBC found that children who experienced frequent, multichannel communication — combining technology-mediated contact with occasional face-to-face contact — showed mental health outcomes statistically indistinguishable from children who were never left behind, while children with infrequent or single-channel contact fared significantly worse (Xie et al., 2024).
- **Caregiving arrangement:** the quality of the substitute caregiving relationship, typically a grandparent, moderates emotional outcomes independently of parental absence itself (Račaitė et al., 2021).
- **Household financial status:** findings are mixed rather than uniformly negative — remittances can improve nutrition, material security and school access, partially offsetting emotional cost, which complicates (without disproving) the team's working hypothesis that financial hardship is the key segmenting variable (Antia et al., 2020). The evidence instead suggests financial status interacts with, rather than substitutes for, the quality and frequency of parent-child contact.
- **Age and duration:** younger children (roughly ages 6–12) and those experiencing longer, more prolonged separation appear to be at greater risk than adolescents or those with shorter separations (Fellmeth et al., 2018).

This section confirms rather than discards the team's working hypothesis: financial difficulty status is a real and evidenced factor that compounds the core problem — adding security, supervision, and education-access vulnerabilities on top of the time/companionship gap present across all segments (Section 3.2 of the Problem Identification Document). This compounding effect is the basis for the team's decision to narrow primary research focus to financially disadvantaged households (Section 1.4), rather than splitting effort evenly across both segments identified in Phase 1. Within that narrowed focus, the evidence further indicates that communication quality and caregiving quality remain the dominant *moderating* variables even where financial hardship is present — meaning they are also the area where an interaction-design intervention has the most plausible leverage, rather than a competing explanation to be tested against financial status.

## 2.4 Existing Interventions and Technology-Mediated Communication

This subsection is the most directly relevant to an interaction design studio project, since it moves the review from public-health outcomes toward design-relevant precedent.

On the intervention side, a community-based programme in rural China — physical "Children's Centres" offering supervised activities and peer support — produced measurable one-year improvements in psychosocial wellbeing and school performance for LBC, demonstrating that structured, non-clinical, community-level support is a viable and effective category of response, not only medical or policy-level intervention (Jiang et al., 2020).

On the technology side, a growing HCI and communication-studies literature examines how migrant families use everyday tools — phone calls, video calls, and messaging apps — to sustain the parent-child relationship across distance. Foundational work on "mobile phone parenting" describes how migrant mothers use mobile technology to reconfigure, rather than replace, parenting practices from a distance (Madianou & Miller, 2011). Within HCI specifically, prior work on supporting parent-child communication in geographically or relationally separated families — for example families separated by divorce — has explored media spaces and remote synchronous interaction as design targets, establishing a design precedent for the kind of interaction the SpyKids team could investigate (Yarosh et al., 2009). Combined with the finding in Section 2.3 that multichannel communication is protective, this literature suggests that the interaction design opportunity is less about proving that technology can help — that is already evidenced — and more about how a tool for this specific population should be designed: for asynchronous versus synchronous contact, for the child's agency versus the parent's, and for the caregiver's role in mediating the exchange.

## 2.5 Gaps in the Literature

Three consistent gaps emerge across the reviewed literature, and each directly shapes the SpyKids research scope in Section 1.4:

- **Outcome-heavy, solution-light:** the literature is dominated by epidemiological and psychological outcome studies (prevalence, risk ratios, correlations) and comparatively thin on evaluated, everyday, non-clinical support tools, particularly ones designed around the child's own agency rather than the parent's or the researcher's.
- **Geographic concentration:** the evidence base is heavily weighted toward China and, secondarily, Southeast Asia; the team is explicit about which cultural context its research scope will draw on, rather than treating findings as universally generalisable.
- **Caregiver and child perspective under-represented in design research:** most technology-focused studies centre the migrant parent's use of communication tools; comparatively few study the left-behind child's or on-the-ground caregiver's experience of the same technology, which is precisely the gap the team's "Who" lens (children, caregivers, wider household) is positioned to address.

## 2.6 Knowledge Gap and Project Positioning

Synthesising the sections above, the literature review identifies a specific and defensible gap for this project to occupy: the evidence base robustly establishes that left-behind children in financially disadvantaged households face a compounded risk profile — the well-evidenced emotional and psychological harms common across the wider LBC population, plus additional security, supervision, and education-access vulnerabilities specific to economic hardship — yet it does not offer well-evaluated, child-centred, everyday interaction design responses built around that compounded profile, particularly outside the dominant Chinese research context.

SpyKids is therefore positioned not to prove that parental absence affects wellbeing — already well evidenced — and not to build a clinical or therapeutic intervention, or an economic/policy intervention targeting household income — both out of scope and outside the team's expertise — but to explore how everyday interaction design can support the quality and meaning of parent–child connection across distance specifically for children in low-income, financially disadvantaged labour-migrant households, for the child, not only the parent, while treating the caregiver as a legitimate and necessary third party in that relationship, rather than an obstacle to route around.

# 3. Research Implementation Plan

The SpyKids team adopts an iterative, evidence-first methodology: problem framing and literature review precede fieldwork, fieldwork precedes synthesis, and synthesis precedes any design concept. This cyclical approach ensures that assessments (the Problem Identification Document, Assessment 1, Assessment 2) are treated as by-products of the actual research work, rather than the focus of the work itself.

## 3.1 Project Aim

The aim of this research is to understand how parental absence affects the emotional wellbeing, guidance, and development of left-behind children in low-income, financially disadvantaged labour-migrant households specifically, and to identify where an interaction design response could plausibly and ethically support the quality of connection between these children and their migrant parents, before any design concept is proposed.

## 3.2 Project Objectives

- **Objective 1 (Problem Understanding):** Formalise and document the team's problem framing — the Who/How/Why/Impact/Risks lenses and the decision to narrow primary focus to financially disadvantaged households — as a reviewable baseline (the Problem Identification Document), explicitly distinguishing assumption from evidence.
- **Objective 2 (Evidence Synthesis):** Complete a literature review that tests the team's Phase 1 assumptions against peer-reviewed evidence, confirming financial disadvantage as a compounding rather than competing factor, and use the findings to refine the research scope and sub-questions.
- **Objective 3 (Fieldwork and Triangulation):** Execute a triangulated Suite of Methods — contextual inquiry, rapid ethnography, digital ethnography, mapping, object inventory and autoethnography — with adult intermediaries, gathering 6–10 participant engagements prioritising financially disadvantaged migrant households, to test the Assumptions Register.
- **Objective 4 (Synthesis and Direction):** Synthesise fieldwork and literature findings into an updated problem framing and a design concept hypothesis for the financially disadvantaged segment — a shared direction for the team's design response, not a finished specification.

## 3.7 Ethics Statement

This research adheres to the University of Queensland's ethical research standards, with particular care given to the vulnerable-population sensitivity of the topic.

**Human Data — No Direct Research Contact with Children:** All primary data collection is conducted with adults — caregivers, educators, and community workers — or secondary and public sources. Left-behind children are not directly interviewed, observed, or otherwise engaged as research participants, in recognition of the ethical sensitivity of researching a vulnerable minor population within a studio-scale, non-clinical project. This scoping decision is treated as fixed (Section 1.4.2) rather than open for revision without a formal ethics review.

**Informed Consent and Confidentiality:** All adult interview participants will be briefed on the voluntary nature of their involvement, the purpose of the research, and the confidentiality of their responses, and will provide informed consent prior to participation, in line with standard qualitative research practice and course ethics requirements.

**Sensitive Sub-Topics:** Because this research now focuses specifically on financially disadvantaged households, the security/supervision-vulnerability and education-access findings identified in Phase 1 (Section 3.2 of the Problem Identification Document) sit at the centre of the study rather than at its margins. These require particularly careful, non-sensationalised handling in both research questions and any future public-facing materials, so the research examines structural and circumstantial factors rather than implying fault on the part of low-income migrant families or their caregiving arrangements.

**Formal Ethics Review Status:** Given that this research does not involve direct contact with children and is conducted at studio-project scale with adult intermediaries, it is treated as a low-risk activity for the purposes of course ethics requirements. Ethics will nonetheless be formally revisited by the team at the Week 4 studio topic (vulnerable populations) and again after Phase 2 data collection, consistent with the Problem Identification Document's gate criteria. Should the project's scope later extend to direct engagement with children, or should design concepts require additional data sensitivity considerations, the team will seek formal guidance from the teaching team on whether HREC (Human Research Ethics Committee) clearance is required.

## 3.8 Research Data Management, Governance, and Reproducibility

All data generated throughout this research, including interview notes, ethnography logs, coded findings, and design journal entries, will be managed in accordance with course expectations for research documentation and version control.

- **Version Control and Reproducibility:** All project documents — the Project Plan, Problem Identification Document, Research Plan, and this proposal — are managed and versioned via a centralised GitHub repository (`DECO2850-7285`). The repository's README serves as a navigation document, using relative links to key folders and documents. This gives the team, and the teaching team where relevant, an auditable record of how the project's understanding of the problem evolved.
- **Document Format:** Key documents are maintained in both Markdown and Word (.docx) form, with Markdown treated as the editable source and Word versions generated programmatically and visually verified, to keep both formats consistent and to support easy sharing and review.
- **Data Handling:** Interview and ethnography data involving identifiable information about adult participants will be anonymised in shared documents and stored securely, consistent with the confidentiality commitments made during informed consent.
- **Design Journal:** Maintained collaboratively in Miro, with a minimum of one entry per member per week, providing a running, timestamped record of the team's evolving understanding — directly supporting the individual design-journal interview (Assessment 3).
