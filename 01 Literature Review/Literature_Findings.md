# Literature Review: Impacts of Inadequate Parental Supervision and Parental Absence on Australian Children

## 1. All-Cause Fatal Unintentional Injury and Parental Loss

Children without adequate parental supervision or long-term parental absence face a substantially higher risk of fatal unintentional injury in Australia. Between 2015 and 2017, injury accounted for 563 deaths among Australian children aged 0–14, representing a mortality rate of 4.1 per 100,000 children. Drowning was one of the leading fatal injury causes, and coronial case reviews consistently identify unsupervised children as a key risk factor for preventable child injury deaths. Young children left at home without adult supervision are significantly more exposed to life-threatening accidents, which contribute to this injury-related child mortality burden (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [AIHW], 2022).

Children experiencing broader forms of parental loss (parental separation, parental death, or parental incarceration) also demonstrate poorer long-term health-related outcomes. By age 17, 37.8% of Australian children were not living with both biological parents; children with parental loss experiences showed double the rate of diagnosable mental health conditions compared with peers living with two biological parents (Breen et al., 2026).

## 2. Near-Drowning and Fatal Drowning

Drowning remains one of the leading preventable fatal injury causes for Australian young children. Across the ten-year period from July 2015 to June 2025, 182 children aged 0–4 died from drowning nationally, averaging approximately 18 fatalities per year, with a crude mortality rate of 1.18 per 100,000 children in this age bracket. Among these pool-related drowning deaths, 78% of children received no direct adult supervision at the time of the incident—a finding highlighting the severe danger of parental or carer absence near water sources.

For children aged 5–14, the average annual drowning death toll was 10 cases, with a fatal drowning rate of 0.35 per 100,000 population; increased independence and reduced adult supervision are documented risk factors for this age group as well. State-level coronial reviews further confirm that insufficient adult supervision contributed to most child drowning fatalities (Royal Life Saving Society Australia, 2025; NSW Child Death Review Team, 2025).

## 3. Entrapment and Accidental Asphyxia

Accidental asphyxia and entrapment deaths, including blind-cord hanging, bedding suffocation, and appliance entrapment, frequently occur when young children play without adult supervision. In Australia, between 2014 and 2023, 112 children aged 0–14 died from unintentional suffocation and strangulation. A large proportion of these fatal incidents happened during periods of reduced or absent caregiver supervision, as children can become trapped quickly with no adult nearby to intervene. For children under 5 years old, suffocation and strangulation rank among the top five unintentional injury-related death causes (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [AIHW], 2024; NSW Child Death Review Team, 2024).

## 4. Falls

Fatal falls represent a meaningful source of unintentional child mortality in Australia. From 2014 to 2023, 87 children aged 0–14 died from fall-related injuries. Many fatal fall incidents take place at home, including falls from windows, balconies, furniture, and stairs. Coronial investigations highlight that insufficient or absent adult supervision is a recurring contributing factor: unsupervised young children may climb onto high surfaces and encounter hazards without timely adult intervention. Fall-related deaths are less common for very young children than drowning or suffocation, yet they carry a high risk of permanent severe disability for non-fatal cases (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [AIHW], 2024; NSW Child Death Review Team, 2024).

## 5. Burns and Scalds

Fatal burns and scalds among Australian children aged 0–14 are relatively uncommon compared with drowning or suffocation, yet these injuries carry a very high risk of permanent disability. Between 2014 and 2023, 39 children aged 0–14 died from fire, burn, and scald-related injuries nationwide. Most burn-related incidents take place inside residential homes, including contact with hot cooking liquids, stoves, heaters, and house fires. Coronial reviews indicate that insufficient adult supervision is a recurring contributing factor: unsupervised young children may pull over hot containers or approach fire hazards with no nearby adult to intervene rapidly. While scalds produce large numbers of non-fatal hospital admissions, fire-related burns account for the majority of burn-related child fatalities (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [AIHW], 2024; NSW Child Death Review Team, 2024).

## 6. Educational Outcomes and Disadvantage

Australian national data shows clear educational gaps for young people who experienced parental absence during childhood:

* **High School Completion:** Among 18–24-year-old young adults who experienced parental separation or divorce in childhood, **62%** completed Year 12, compared with **77%** of peers who lived with both biological parents (a **15 percentage-point gap**).
* **Higher Education Attainment:** After adjusting for age, individuals with childhood parental separation were **28% less likely** to obtain a bachelor’s degree or higher qualification.
* **Out-of-Home Care:** For children placed in out-of-home care due to long-term parental inability to provide care, only **57%** of care leavers aged 18–25 completed Year 12, versus the national average of **85%**.
* **Academic Benchmarks (NAPLAN):** Only **69%** of Year 9 students in out-of-home care met the national minimum standard in reading, compared with **93%** of all Australian Year 9 students (ABS, 2010; AIHW, 2022).
* **Parental Incarceration:** For children whose parent is incarcerated, caregiver-reported survey evidence indicates roughly half experience regular school absence, further undermining learning progress (Hannan et al., 2026).
