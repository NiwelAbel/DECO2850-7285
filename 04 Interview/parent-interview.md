# Parent Interview on Communication Between Migrant Parents and Left-Behind Children

## Interview Overview

This edited interview transcript is primarily based on the parent interview and project description in the Project Proposal.
It explores the communication experiences of a migrant-worker parent whose child lives in their hometown with grandparents.
The interview focuses on communication frequency, common conversation topics, barriers to meaningful interaction, and the parent's response to the proposed companion doll.
The participant's identity is kept anonymous.

## Participant Profile

- Role: Migrant-worker parent
- Family situation: Lives and works in another city for most of the year
- Child's living situation: Lives in the hometown and is mainly cared for by grandparents
- Main communication methods: Phone calls and video calls

## Interview Transcript

**Interviewer:** Could you briefly describe your current family situation?

**Parent:** I work in another city for most of the year.
My child stays in our hometown and is mainly looked after by their grandparents.
I usually return home during holidays or when something important happens, but most of the time we live apart.

**Interviewer:** How often do you communicate with your child?

**Parent:** It is not very regular.
Sometimes we talk two or three times a week, but sometimes we only talk once a week.
It depends on my work schedule.
If I finish work late, my child may already be asleep.
Sometimes I want to call, but I feel tired after work and do not know what to talk about.

**Interviewer:** What do you usually discuss during phone or video calls?

**Parent:** We mostly talk about simple things.
I ask whether my child has eaten, finished their homework, listened to their grandparents, or had a good day at school.
Sometimes I also ask about exam results.
The conversation often feels like I am checking on my child instead of having a natural conversation.

**Interviewer:** Do you feel that you understand your child's daily life?

**Parent:** Not very well.
I learn some basic information from the grandparents, but I do not know many details.
For example, I may not know what happened at school, who my child played with, or whether something made them happy or upset.
When I call, I often want to know more, but my child does not always take the initiative to share.

**Interviewer:** Why do you think your child does not share very much?

**Parent:** My child may feel that there is nothing special to talk about.
Because we do not live together, we gradually have fewer shared experiences and conversation topics.
I also often ask about schoolwork first, so my child may think that I only care about homework and grades.
Sometimes I can tell that my child wants to say something but does not know how to begin.

**Interviewer:** What difficulties do you experience when trying to communicate?

**Parent:** Time is the biggest difficulty.
My work is busy, so I cannot always call at a fixed time.
My child also does not have a personal phone and usually needs to use a grandparent's phone.
Communication therefore depends on whether the phone and the grandparent are available.
Video calls can also be difficult because the internet connection is sometimes poor, or my child feels shy in front of the camera.

**Interviewer:** Are existing tools such as phone calls, WeChat, and video calls enough?

**Parent:** They are useful, but they are not enough.
They allow us to contact each other, but they do not automatically make our conversations better.
Even during a video call, I often ask questions and my child gives short answers.
It can feel more like checking on the child than genuinely talking together.

**Interviewer:** What type of communication would you prefer?

**Parent:** I hope my child can share more small moments from daily life.
These could include something that happened at school, something that made them happy, or something that made them feel upset.
I would also like to respond when I have free time instead of missing the moment because I am working.
If I could see or hear small parts of my child's day, I would know how to have a more meaningful conversation with them.

**Interviewer:** What do you think about a companion doll that lets children record audio or video messages for their parents?

**Parent:** I think it could be helpful.
A child may feel more relaxed speaking to a familiar doll than making a direct phone call.
If my child could press a simple button and record something whenever they wanted, sharing might become easier.
Receiving the recording on my phone would also help me understand what my child is experiencing.

**Interviewer:** Would you be willing to reply using voice or short video messages?

**Parent:** Yes.
A voice message would be especially convenient because it is quick to record during a work break.
If my child shared something specific, I could reply directly to that experience.
This would be more natural than asking the same general questions during every call.

**Interviewer:** Do you have any concerns about the companion doll?

**Parent:** Privacy is very important.
The doll should never record automatically, and the child should always know when the microphone or camera is active.
Parents should not be able to turn on the camera or microphone remotely because that would make the product feel like a monitoring device.
The product should help the family communicate without making the child feel watched.

**Interviewer:** What functions would be useful for you and your child?

**Parent:** The doll should have simple physical buttons because children should not need to follow complicated steps.
It could use a gentle light or sound to tell the child when a reply arrives.
The parent application could also remind parents to reply when they are busy.
However, the child should not be told that a parent has failed to reply because that could cause disappointment or worry.

**Interviewer:** Overall, what problem could this design help solve?

**Parent:** I think it could help parents and children develop more shared topics.
If the child can share daily moments first, the parent will have something specific to respond to.
This could make communication feel more natural and help parents remain involved in their child's life even when they live far away.

## Findings Connected to the Project Proposal

1. Different daily schedules make regular real-time communication difficult.
2. Limited access to a personal phone creates an additional communication barrier for the child.
3. Existing conversations often focus on food, homework, behaviour, and grades.
4. The parent wants to understand ordinary details from the child's daily life.
5. Asynchronous voice and video messages could create more shared conversation topics.
6. Simple physical controls are important for children's usability.
7. Recording must always be initiated by the child and clearly indicated.
8. Remote camera or microphone activation should not be available.
9. Parent reminders should encourage replies without placing emotional pressure on the child.
10. The companion doll should support human connection rather than act as a monitoring device or replace parental care.
