# Caregiver Interview

## Interview Questions

1. **Can you describe your experience caring for the child while their parent or parents were away for work?**

2. **What do you think were the child’s biggest emotional or social challenges during this period?**

3. **How did you usually support the child when they were upset, needed advice, or had problems at school or with friends?**

4. **How involved were the child’s parents while they were away?**

5. **What kinds of challenges did you personally face as the primary caregiver?**

6. **Based on your experience, what support would help caregivers and left-behind children the most?**

---

# Interview Responses

## Caregiver 1

**Gender:** Female

### Question 1
**Can you describe your experience caring for the child while their parent or parents were away for work?**

I have looked after my granddaughter since she was about six. At first, it was difficult because suddenly I was responsible for almost everything her parents normally did.

Every morning I prepare breakfast and make sure she gets to school. After school, I prepare dinner, remind her about homework, and make sure she goes to bed on time.

My daily life became centred around her. Before, I only needed to look after myself and the house, but now I have to think about her school schedule, health, meals and everything else.

I don't mind doing these things because she is my granddaughter. The difficult part is that sometimes I feel like I am trying to be both a grandmother and a parent.

---

### Question 2
**What do you think were the child's biggest emotional or social challenges during this period?**

When she was younger, she asked about her mother quite often. Sometimes she would ask when her parents were coming home.

As she got older, she stopped asking as much. But I don't think that means she stopped missing them.

Sometimes after speaking to her parents she becomes very quiet. She doesn't tell me she is sad, so I have to guess from her behaviour.

I also notice it during school activities. When other children's parents attend something and hers cannot come, she doesn't complain, but I can see she is disappointed.

She is very independent now, but sometimes I worry that she has learned to keep too many things to herself.

---

### Question 3
**How did you usually support the child when they were upset, needed advice, or had problems?**

Usually, I make sure she knows that I am there.

If she is upset, I might make her something she likes to eat or sit beside her and ask what happened. For practical problems, I can normally help.

But emotional problems are more difficult for me.

When she talks about friendships or something happening at school, sometimes I don't know what advice to give. School is also very different now compared with when I was young.

As her homework becomes harder, there are many things I cannot help her with either.

Sometimes I wish her parents could be involved more because there are things a grandmother cannot replace.

---

### Question 4
**How involved were the child's parents while they were away?**

They care about her very much and usually call several times each week, but their work can make it difficult to have a regular time.

The problem is that the conversations are often very similar.

They ask:

- "Did you finish your homework?"
- "What did you eat?"
- "Are you listening to Grandma?"
- "How were your grades?"

My granddaughter usually says:

- "Yes."
- "Good."
- "I'm fine."

Sometimes I think they know whether she completed her homework, but they don't really know what happened during her day.

When something small happens—maybe she had an argument with a friend or did something she was proud of—her parents might never hear about it.

---

### Question 5
**What kinds of challenges did you personally face as the primary caregiver?**

Education is probably one of the biggest challenges.

When she was young, I could help with simple homework. Now the schoolwork is becoming more difficult, and I don't always understand it.

Technology is another problem. I can use WeChat, make calls and send simple voice messages, but when an application asks me to create an account, change settings or update something, I get confused.

There is also a generation gap.

I know how to make sure she eats properly, stays warm and goes to school. But sometimes I don't know how to talk to her about her feelings.

That makes me worry that I am taking care of her physically but maybe not always giving her everything she needs emotionally.

---

### Question 6
**Based on your experience, what support would help caregivers and left-behind children the most?**

I think the most important thing is helping parents stay involved in the child's normal life.

Calling is good, but parents should not only ask about school and grades. Children need to feel that their parents know them.

It would be useful to have something simple where parents could leave a voice message, encouragement or greeting before work, and the child could listen to it later.

For grandparents, technology needs to be very simple. If I have to set up many things every day, I probably won't use it.

Schools could also help more. Teachers see children every day, so they might notice things grandparents don't notice.

I think parents, grandparents and teachers need to communicate more instead of each person only knowing one part of the child's life.

# Caregiver 2

**Gender:** Male

### Question 1
**Can you describe your experience caring for the children while their parents were away for work?**

My wife and I have looked after our two grandchildren for several years.

Our lives became much busier. Everything has to follow the children's schedule—school, meals, homework and activities.

Because there are two children, there is always something happening.

My grandson is older, so he started helping with his younger sister. He walks with her to school sometimes and helps her with homework.

At first, I thought this was good because he was becoming responsible.

But as he got older, I realised we sometimes relied on him too much. He is still a child himself, and he shouldn't always feel responsible for solving his sister's problems.

---

### Question 2
**What do you think were the children's biggest emotional or social challenges?**

My grandson doesn't show his emotions very much.

If he misses his parents, he usually doesn't say it. He goes outside, plays basketball or spends time with his friends.

The biggest change I noticed is that he became very independent.

That sounds positive, but sometimes it goes too far. If he has a problem, his first reaction is usually:

> "I can deal with it."

My granddaughter is different. She asks about her parents more often and becomes excited when they call.

I think both children miss their parents, but they show it differently.

My grandson hides it by being independent, while my granddaughter expresses it more directly.

---

### Question 3
**How did you usually support the children when they were upset or needed advice?**

For practical problems, I can help.

If there is something wrong at school, I talk to the teacher. If they need something, I try to solve it.

I also encourage my grandson to talk to me rather than keeping everything to himself.

But there are definitely situations where I am unsure.

Children today experience different social pressures than we did. Sometimes they have problems with friends or school, and I don't completely understand why it feels so serious to them.

My grandson often talks to his friends instead.

I don't think that is necessarily bad, but sometimes I wonder if there are problems he doesn't tell any adult about.

---

### Question 4
**How involved were the children's parents while they were away?**

They try to stay involved, but their work schedules are difficult.

Sometimes they can video call several times in one week. Other times they are very busy and don't call for a few days.

The children understand that their parents are working.

Actually, my grandson understands it too well sometimes. He will say:

> "Don't call Dad, he's working."

Even when something happens, that means the parents sometimes don't hear about things until later.

For example, if he does well at school, his parents might not know until I tell them several days later.

When they do call, they usually ask about:

- School
- Behaviour
- Health
- Whether the children are listening to us

I think they want to know more, but sometimes they don't know what to ask because they haven't experienced the small things that happened during the week.

---

### Question 5
**What kinds of challenges did you personally face as the primary caregiver?**

The biggest challenge is probably balancing responsibility.

I want the children to become independent, but I don't want my grandson to feel that he has to become another adult in the family.

Education is another challenge. School has changed a lot, and sometimes the homework is difficult for us to understand.

Technology is not too bad for me. I can use a smartphone, WeChat and video calls.

The bigger technology problem is coordination.

The children might be free when their parents are working. When the parents finally have time, the children might be doing homework or sleeping.

So even though everyone has a smartphone, finding the right time to communicate is still difficult.

---

### Question 6
**Based on your experience, what support would help caregivers and left-behind children the most?**

I think we need more flexible ways for parents and children to communicate.

It shouldn't always depend on everyone being available for a video call at exactly the same time.

If something good happens at school, the child should be able to share it immediately. The parent could respond later when they finish work.

It would also be good if the children could hear something from their parents during the day—a short message, encouragement, reminder or even just their parent's voice.

Schools could also communicate more with parents who live away from home. At the moment, grandparents often become the middle person passing information between teachers, children and parents.

Most importantly, I don't think every left-behind child needs the same kind of help.

Some children need someone to talk to. Some need help with school.

Others, like my grandson, might actually need adults to tell them:

> "You don't have to solve everything yourself."
